## The "Tapestry" Game Soul

**A Framework for Institutional Inertia, Map-Painting, and the Generative Dynasty**

The soul of games like *Crusader Kings*, *Stellaris*, *Europa Universalis*, *Victoria*, and *RimWorld* (at a macro scale) can be distilled into a single philosophy: "You direct the stream, but you do not steer the boat."

On the surface, these games look like impenetrable spreadsheets stapled to a world map. Beneath the surface lies a ruthless generative story engine. The player plays as a ruler, an empire, or a dynasty, but they do not possess absolute control. Power is delegated, subjects have their own traits, and every order is filtered through layers of institutional friction. It is a game about watching a meticulously laid plan fall apart because your spymaster hates your heir.

### Components

* **components/living_world.txt:** Vassals, factions, and neighboring empires have their own internal agendas, economies, and grudges. They act whether you prompt them or not.
* **components/failure_as_content.txt:** Losing a war doesn't end the game; it creates a revenge arc for your grandson to inherit.
* **components/scarcity_economy.txt:** The real scarce resources are abstract political capital: influence, legitimacy, prestige, and mana.
* **components/systemic_consistency.txt:** The AI plays by the exact same rules as the player. They suffer the same attrition, the same rebellions, and the same marriage logic.

### Signature Pillar 1: The Friction of Command

In a traditional strategy game, you click a tank, right-click an enemy, and the tank fires. In a Tapestry-style game, orders are suggestions made to a system that might refuse them.

**Design Rules:**

* **People Are the Machine:** The empire is made of characters (or factions, or estates). To raise an army, you must ask the nobles who own the land. If the nobles hate you, you get no army.

* **Action Requires Justification:** A player cannot simply declare war because they want land. They must possess a *casus belli* (a legal or religious justification). The rules apply to the ruler, and breaking them incurs massive institutional penalties (tyranny, aggressive expansion).

### Signature Pillar 2: The Succession Crisis

The player is not an immortal god. The entity they control will die, and the transition of power is the central tension of the game.

**Design Rules:**

* **The Rules Change with the Ruler:** When the current leader dies, the empire remains, but the network of loyalty resets. The new heir inherits the land, but not the respect. A stable empire can fracture into civil war purely because the new king is a coward.

* **The Unwanted Heir:** The player must often play as a character they did not optimize and do not like. The system forces them to adapt their playstyle to the flaws of their current avatar.

### Signature Pillar 3: The Map Is the Ledger

Progress is fundamentally territorial and geographical. The story of the game is written in how the borders shift over time.

**Design Rules:**

* **Painting the Map:** The visual reward for hours of spreadsheet optimization is seeing your faction's color spread across the geography. The map must clearly display political, religious, and cultural borders.

* **The Ripple Effect:** If two AI empires go to war on the other side of the map, it should eventually affect the player (trade routes collapse, refugees arrive, a massive blob threatens the balance of power).

### Variants & Exceptions

* *Stellaris* replaces dynasties with species and political ethics. The succession crisis is replaced by factional demands and elections, but the friction of command remains.
* *Victoria* replaces dynastic characters with population demographics (POPs). The player's enemy isn't an angry duke, but a rising middle class demanding voting rights that conflict with the player's economic goals.
* *Mount & Blade* translates this soul into a third-person action game. You command armies in person, but the kingdom-level map-painting, vassal management, and defection mechanics are pure Tapestry.

### What This Soul Costs

* **The UI Tax:** Exposing thousands of interrelated variables (loyalty, tax rates, cultural drift) without overwhelming the player requires massive UI engineering. Tooltips nested inside tooltips become mandatory.
* **Performance Degradation:** Simulating the daily lives, genetics, and wealth of 20,000 AI characters will eventually bring any CPU to its knees. Late-game lag is a notorious genre staple.

### How It Fails

* **Spreadsheet Blindness:** The numbers go up, but no stories emerge. *Symptom:* the game feels like a math problem rather than an empire. *Fix:* ensure that systemic events are translated into human-readable narrative pop-ups (for example, "Your brother was caught stealing").
* **The Omnipotent Ruler:** The player has too much direct control, and subordinates never disobey. *Symptom:* the game becomes too easy once the economy snowballs. *Fix:* introduce institutional inertia. The larger the empire gets, the harder it should be to steer.

### Neighboring Souls

* **Dwarven:** Both rely heavily on system-authored stories and generative chaos. Dwarven operates at a micro, granular level (individual limbs and meals); Tapestry operates at a macro, institutional level (borders and marriages).
* **Casebook:** Both use an Armchair Intrigue tone. Casebook asks you to passively solve a puzzle; Tapestry asks you to actively manage a chaotic system.

### The "Tapestry" Litmus Test

| Check | Question | Correct Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Friction** | If the player orders a subordinate to do something against their own interests, will they hesitate or refuse? | **Yes.** You manage the system; you do not possess mind control. |
| **The Justification** | Can the player attack a peaceful neighbor simply because they want the territory, without any systemic pushback? | **No.** Action requires a justification or incurs a heavy penalty. |
| **The Succession** | If the current leader dies, does the game continue under new, more difficult political circumstances? | **Yes.** The empire remains, but the loyalties reset. |
